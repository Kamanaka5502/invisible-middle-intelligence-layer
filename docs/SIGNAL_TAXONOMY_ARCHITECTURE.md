# Signal Taxonomy Architecture

## Purpose

Signal Taxonomy Architecture structures the intake layer of the Invisible Middle Intelligence Layer.

It exists because visible signals are not meaning by themselves.

A repeated check, delayed action, override, handoff failure, clarification request, or escalation loop does not automatically prove one condition.

The same signal may indicate different structural conditions depending on context, evidence strength, timing, user group, workflow coherence, ownership clarity, and trust state.

## Core Rule

```text
Signal visibility ≠ structural meaning
```

The layer must interpret signals through context before assigning operational significance.

## Signal Families

### 1. Behavioural Signals

Observed human interaction patterns inside or around the system.

Examples:

- repeated checking
- reopening completed work
- navigation looping
- hesitation before completion
- task abandonment
- repeated revalidation
- delayed progression
- avoidance
- switching away from the official workflow

Possible structural meanings:

- trust instability
- weak closure
- predictability instability
- interpretive overload
- unresolved ambiguity
- context fragmentation
- cognitive interaction strain

### 2. Workflow Signals

Operational movement patterns across steps, roles, handoffs, and process states.

Examples:

- handoff failure
- rework
- duplicated intervention
- delayed escalation
- repeated reassessment
- unresolved status states
- transition ambiguity
- informal workaround creation
- parallel tracking outside the system

Possible structural meanings:

- workflow incoherence
- ownership drift
- closure failure
- threshold ambiguity
- containment instability
- context continuity failure
- escalation inflation

### 3. Communication Signals

Meaning instability expressed through human communication.

Examples:

- repeated clarification requests
- reassurance seeking
- ownership uncertainty
- repeated context explanation
- defensive documentation
- repeated confirmation requests
- uncertainty about next steps
- explanation fatigue

Possible structural meanings:

- interpretive instability
- trust instability
- threshold ambiguity
- context fragmentation
- authority burden
- closure weakness
- confidence degradation

### 4. AI Interaction Signals

Signals produced when AI-supported systems create hidden afterwork or confidence instability.

Examples:

- repeated human override
- low-confidence output patterns
- re-prompting loops
- output rejection
- ignored recommendations
- unexplained recommendation rejection
- human verification dependency
- confidence interpretation burden
- AI-generated output requiring substantial rewrite

Possible structural meanings:

- AI burden transfer
- hidden verification burden
- trust instability around AI output
- authority shift
- context collapse
- human oversight ambiguity
- automation afterwork

### 5. Governance Signals

Signals that indicate weak thresholds, unclear responsibility, or unstable escalation structure.

Examples:

- threshold inconsistency
- unclear escalation timing
- escalation inflation
- ownership drift
- duplicated authority
- informal assumption of responsibility
- unresolved sign-off
- uncertainty around seriousness
- repeated discretionary decision-making

Possible structural meanings:

- governance weakness
- authority burden
- threshold ambiguity
- escalation instability
- accountability fragmentation
- consequence uncertainty

### 6. Temporal Signals

Patterns that become meaningful over time.

Examples:

- recurrence
- persistence
- acceleration
- failed recovery
- repeated reappearance after intervention
- burden accumulation trajectory
- escalation pattern development
- widening participation loss

Possible structural meanings:

- structural condition strengthening
- containment degradation
- unresolved root cause
- trust deterioration
- workflow instability
- flourishing degradation

## Contextual Interpretation Variables

Signals should be interpreted against:

- workflow conditions
- ownership structures
- threshold definitions
- predictability conditions
- system coherence
- closure architecture
- communication pathways
- AI confidence architecture
- escalation logic
- continuity conditions
- user group and interaction sensitivity
- consequence level

## Evidence Strength

Signals should be assigned an evidence strength level.

| Strength | Meaning | Use |
|---|---|---|
| Informational | Single weak indicator | Observe only |
| Emerging | Multiple aligned weak indicators | Early pattern detection |
| Moderate | Repeated aligned indicators across time or domains | Operational attention warranted |
| Strong | Persistent multi-domain evidence | Response guidance appropriate |
| Critical | Persistent structural pattern with measurable consequence | Governance response, redesign, or optional boundary handoff may be required |

## Example: Repeated Checking

Repeated checking alone may represent healthy diligence.

Repeated checking plus unresolved completion states may indicate closure weakness.

Repeated checking plus reassurance seeking and duplicate validation may indicate trust instability.

Repeated checking plus AI override and low-confidence output may indicate AI burden transfer.

Repeated checking plus unclear ownership and escalation hesitation may indicate structural governance instability.

## Safeguard

The purpose is not to pathologise behaviour.

The purpose is to determine whether hidden compensatory burden is structurally present.

Visible strain should be interpreted through system conditions before individual deficit assumptions.
